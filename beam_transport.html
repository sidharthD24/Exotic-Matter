Computational and experimental studies of negative hydrogen ion beam transport, space-charge interactions, gas neutralization and beam evolution through the neutral beamline.

BEAM TRANSPORT

Understanding the evolution of an extracted ion beam

After extraction and acceleration, the negative hydrogen ion beam propagates through the transport line where its spatial distribution is governed by space-charge forces, beam divergence, electromagnetic fields and interactions with the background gas.

The transport studies focus on understanding how the extracted beam evolves along the beamline and how these effects influence beam uniformity, divergence and transmission.

TRANSPORT PHYSICS

From extraction to beam propagation

The extracted H⁻ beam consists of charged particles propagating at high velocity through the accelerator and transport system. As the beam propagates, the mutual electrostatic interaction between particles modifies its transverse distribution.

Numerical modelling is used to investigate the resulting beam expansion and divergence and to identify the contribution of collective space-charge effects to beam transport.

01

Beamlet Propagation

Evolution of individual extracted beamlets through the accelerator and transport region, including transverse expansion and divergence.

02

Space-Charge Effects

Investigation of the collective electrostatic interaction between charged particles and its influence on beam expansion and transport.

03

Beam Profile Evolution

Spatial evolution of the transverse beam profile along the transport line and its comparison with experimentally measured beam distributions.

04

Beam Scraping

Study of beam interaction with apertures and scrapers and the resulting modification of the transmitted beam distribution.

GAS NEUTRALIZATION

Converting the charged beam into a neutral particle beam

A negative hydrogen ion beam cannot be directly injected into a fusion plasma because the charged particles would be strongly influenced by magnetic fields. The beam is therefore passed through a gas neutralizer, where collisions between H⁻ ions and gas molecules lead to electron stripping and the formation of fast neutral hydrogen atoms.

The neutralization process is inherently probabilistic. The final composition of the beam therefore depends on collision probability, gas density, neutralizer length, particle energy and the relevant interaction cross sections.

NEUTRALIZATION PROCESS

Charge-changing collisions inside the neutralizer

The negative hydrogen ions undergo charge-changing interactions while travelling through the neutralizer. These collisions can strip the extra electron from H⁻, producing energetic neutral hydrogen atoms.

At the same time, competing processes can produce positive ions or lead to further interactions of the particles. A realistic description therefore requires consideration of the gas density and energy-dependent collision cross sections.

01

Gas Neutralizer

The accelerated H⁻ beam passes through a controlled gas target where charge-changing collisions convert a fraction of the ions into energetic neutral hydrogen atoms.

02

Electron Stripping

Collision-induced electron stripping changes H⁻ into neutral hydrogen, forming the principal mechanism responsible for neutral beam generation.

03

Neutral Fraction

The fraction of particles converted into neutrals depends on gas density, interaction cross sections, particle energy and the effective neutralizer length.

04

Residual Charged Beam

The neutralizer output contains neutral particles together with residual charged species, which can subsequently be separated using an electrostatic residual ion dump.

MONTE CARLO MODELLING

Simulating charge-changing collisions statistically

Monte Carlo methods provide a natural framework for modelling the stochastic interactions between beam particles and background gas molecules.

Individual particles are propagated through the neutralizer while collision probabilities are evaluated using the local gas density, particle velocity and relevant energy-dependent cross sections. The statistical ensemble of particle histories can then be used to determine the evolution of the charged and neutral populations.

MONTE CARLO SIMULATIONS

Following individual particles through the neutralizer

The Monte Carlo model follows individual beam particles through the gas target and determines their charge state after successive collision events.

By repeating the calculation for a large ensemble of particles, the model provides the spatial evolution of the H⁻, H⁰ and other charged populations along the neutralizer.

01

Particle Histories

Individual Monte Carlo particle histories are followed through the gas target to determine the occurrence and location of charge-changing collisions.

02

Charge-State Evolution

The population of H⁻, H⁰ and other charge states is tracked as a function of position along the neutralizer.

03

Neutralization Fraction

The calculated neutral fraction can be evaluated for different gas pressures, neutralizer lengths and beam energies.

04

Particle Population

Spatial distributions of the different particle populations provide information about charge conversion throughout the neutralizer.

PHYSICAL MODEL

Linking gas density, cross sections and beam energy

The probability of a collision over a small propagation distance can be related to the local target density and the relevant interaction cross section.

P = 1 − exp[−ng σ(E) Δz]

where ng is the background gas density, σ(E) is the energy-dependent collision cross section, and Δz is the propagation distance.

The accumulated collision probability determines the evolution of the surviving H⁻ population and the production of neutral particles along the neutralizer.

dI / dz = −ng σ(E) I

This provides a basis for connecting the Monte Carlo particle histories with macroscopic beam neutralization behaviour.

BEAMLINE PHYSICS

From charged-particle transport to neutral beam formation

Beam transport and neutralization are closely connected stages of a negative-ion neutral beam injector. The extracted H⁻ beam must first be transported with acceptable divergence and transmission before entering the gas neutralizer.

The neutralization stage then changes the charge-state composition of the beam through stochastic collisions. The combined modelling framework therefore provides a route to study the evolution of the beam from extraction, through transport and gas interaction, to the final neutral beam.

ONGOING WORK

Towards integrated beamline modelling

Future development will focus on coupling the beam transport and neutralization models to obtain a more complete description of the negative-ion beamline.

This includes incorporating realistic gas-density distributions, energy-dependent cross sections, secondary particle production and spatially varying neutralization conditions.

The resulting particle-based model can be used to investigate the relationship between beam transport, particle losses, neutralization efficiency and the characteristics of the resulting neutral beam.
